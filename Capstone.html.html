Ranking Content Refresh Opportunities: A Leakage-Aware Classifier for SEO Intervention Prioritization

A page-level content-opportunity scoring model that learns which pages benefit from editorial refresh — evaluated with a grouped, client-aware split to avoid the traps that make most SEO ML retrospectives look better than they are.

Author Muhammad Abdul Rehman Saleem
Role ML Intern, FlyRank AI
Lane Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring
Week 08 · Capstone Submission
01 Abstract

Five sentences.

▲ Abstract

Question. Can a supervised model reliably rank the pages most in need of an editorial refresh — better than the transparent heuristics an editor could apply by hand? Data. Aggregated page-level snapshots from the FlyRank ML Internship dataset, joined via DuckDB over Hugging Face, with all client-identifying and label-derived fields excluded. Method. A Random Forest classifier trained on five position-, CTR-, and volume-based features, benchmarked against a transparent position-and-clicks heuristic, and validated with a five-fold GroupKFold split grouped by client_id to prevent leakage across clients. Result. On the same grouped split, the model reaches an F1 of 0.74 and ROC-AUC of 0.82 against a baseline of F1 0.38 and ROC-AUC 0.50 — with the caveat that positive-class base rate is 28.5%, and observed lift is a decision-support signal, not a causal claim. Output. A ranked action playbook that groups scored pages into four editor-facing tiers (refresh, metadata review, monitor, deprioritize), designed to replace ad-hoc weekly triage with a defensible, reproducible surface.

02 Introduction

The decision this work supports.

Every week, an editorial team at a search-visibility company faces the same question: which pages, out of thousands under management, deserve a human's attention? This paper builds a scoring layer for that decision.

Unit of analysis, output, and cost

  • Unit of analysis: a client-page pair at a weekly snapshot boundary.
  • Output: a probability score for the binary label needs_action, converted into a ranked list and four editor-facing tiers.
  • Action a human takes: refresh, metadata review, monitor, or deprioritize — assigned at the tier level, never auto-executed.
  • Cost of a wrong call: a false negative (missed decaying page) compounds into slow, quiet traffic loss; a false positive burns scarce editor hours on stable content that did not need work.

Why ML — and where it stops

A simple "position dropped by more than X" rule already exists in most SEO workflows and it is fair, transparent, and cheap. It also misses interactions — a page whose position is stable but whose CTR has quietly decayed rarely trips a position-only threshold, and volume alone gives false signals when position is unstable. A model that jointly weighs position dynamics, CTR decay, and volume can, in principle, do better. This paper measures whether it actually does, on a leakage-controlled split, and refuses to make any claim beyond that.

▲ SCOPE NOTE

This work is decision-support, not automation. Every tier is designed to route pages to an editor for review, never to publish or de-index anything on its own.

03 Data & Scope

What we used — and, more importantly, what we excluded.

Release & access

The full warehouse release lives on the FlyRank ML Internship gated Hugging Face card. Aggregations were performed in DuckDB directly over hf:// URIs, matching the pattern in starter notebook 03. Feature construction happens page-side, not query-side, to avoid pulling identifying query strings into memory at any point.

Date window & sampling

  • Rolling 90-day window per snapshot boundary for feature computation.
  • Labels defined on a forward 7-day window strictly disjoint from the feature window.
  • Clients with fewer than 50 tracked pages excluded from evaluation — noted as a selection-bias contributor in the Limitations section.

Deliberate exclusions (and why)

▲ LEAKAGE-SENSITIVE FIELDS · REMOVED

trend_direction and trend_pct are label-derived — they encode the future the model is being asked to predict. Excluded from features entirely.

client_id is pseudonymous but powerful. Used only as the grouping key in the split; never passed to the model as a feature.

Client domain names, page URLs, and raw query strings are excluded end-to-end. Nothing in work/ can be reversed back to a real client.

Public-safety confirmation

Consistent with the capstone's public rule, this paper contains no client names, no domain strings, no raw query text, no exported credentials, and no claim about Google's algorithm. The word Google appears nowhere in a causal frame in any section.

04 Methodology

Features, label, baseline, split.

Target definition

The binary label needs_action is set to 1 for a client-page pair at snapshot t if the forward 7-day window shows either (a) a sustained position degradation beyond the client's own noise band, or (b) a CTR decay at stable position that a manual review would flag. All label computation happens strictly on the forward window and is never exposed to the model at training time.

Feature set

Five features, all computed on the trailing 90-day window:

  • position_delta — change in average rank position over the trailing window.
  • ctr_decay_rate — slope of CTR over the trailing window, normalized per page.
  • impression_volume — trailing 90-day impressions, log-scaled.
  • clicks_30d — trailing 30-day clicks.
  • avg_position — trailing average position, used as a coarse regime signal.

Deliberately left out: trend_direction, trend_pct (label-derived); client_id, domain strings, URL strings (identity leakage); any query-level fields (public-safety).

Baseline

A transparent rule any editor can reproduce without ML:

▲ HEURISTIC BASELINE

flag if (position_delta > +2 rank positions) AND (clicks_30d dropped ≥ 20%)

This is intentionally not a strawman. It is a rule an editor could — and often does — apply from a spreadsheet, and it is the fair thing to beat.

Model

Random Forest Classifier (sklearn.ensemble.RandomForestClassifier) with n_estimators=200, max_depth=10, class_weight='balanced', random_state=42. Chosen for interpretability of feature importances, resilience to modest class imbalance, and the fact that we should be able to explain why a page was scored high to an editor.

Validation split

GroupKFold, k = 5, grouped by client_id. Same client's pages never appear in both training and evaluation folds. This is deliberately stricter than a random split — a random split would inflate scores because the model would memorize client-level regularities that do not generalize to new clients.

Leakage checks performed

  • Label window (forward 7 days) strictly disjoint from feature window (trailing 90 days).
  • Label-derived fields (trend_direction, trend_pct) audited and dropped from the feature frame before fit().
  • Pseudonymous client_id validated as absent from the feature frame at train time (asserted in-notebook).
  • Metrics reported on the same grouped split for both baseline and model.

Error analysis — the short version that beats a big table

False positives concentrate on pages with unusually high impression_volume paired with a stable avg_position. The model overweighted volume as an intervention signal on those pages; an editor reviewing the tier-2 list should discount high-volume-plus-stable-position pages by default.

False negatives concentrate on slow-decay pages where position_delta is small but ctr_decay_rate is quietly negative. The model discounted these until CTR decay accumulated enough; a monitoring tier catches them earlier than the model does alone.

▲ HONEST SURPRISE (NEGATIVE FINDING)

High impression_volume alone did not correlate with content recovery without position stability. In plain words: a page can be losing traffic even while raking in impressions — the intervention signal lives in position dynamics, not raw traffic volume. This directly contradicts the intuition that "big-traffic pages are the ones to save."

06 Limitations & Honest Framing

What this work cannot claim.

Language, on the record

This paper uses the following words on purpose:

observed measured directional decision-support associated with

And deliberately avoids these:

predicts Google's algorithm causes traffic recovery proves refresh works will improve rankings

What we did not do

  • No live A/B test. Every number in this paper is retrospective, measured on historical snapshots. A live intervention experiment would be required to make any causal claim.
  • No claim about Google's ranking algorithm. The model has no visibility into Google's internal signals — it learns from what a page's public-observable metrics did, nothing more.
  • Selection bias from the <50-page exclusion. Small clients are absent from evaluation; performance on very-small accounts is not measured here.
  • Client heterogeneity remains. Client-grouped splits reduce within-client leakage but cannot fix genuine cross-client distribution shift for very unusual verticals.

What "F1 = 0.74" is — and isn't

0.74 is a ranking quality signal on a task with 28.5% base rate. It says the model separates the classes far better than the transparent rule on the same split. It does not say that acting on 100% of the flagged pages will recover 74% of the potential traffic. Any traffic-impact number would require the live experiment this paper does not run.

07 Ranked Recommendations

The editor's playbook.

Model scores are converted into four editor-facing tiers, ranked by opportunity strength. Each tier ships with a review action and a monitoring window — no tier auto-publishes anything.

01Tier

Refresh candidates — position drop + CTR decay

Pages showing joint signals: sustained position_delta and negative ctr_decay_rate. Route to editorial for content + metadata refresh; monitor a 14-day window post-intervention.

≥ 0.70 High confidence
02Tier

Metadata review — impressions-rich, low CTR

Pages with high impression_volume and depressed CTR at stable position. Prioritize a titles / snippets review before a full content refresh.

0.50 – 0.70 Medium confidence
03Tier

Monitor — stable position, gradual signal

Slow-decay pages the model itself sometimes misses. Route to a passive monitor list; re-score weekly, escalate on decay slope acceleration.

0.30 – 0.50 Directional
04Tier

Deprioritize — sample audit only

No strong intervention signal in the current window. Sample a small percentage each week to catch drift; do not spend editor cycles here.

< 0.30 Low priority

How an editor uses this tomorrow morning

  1. Open the ranked CSV output from the notebook — one row per client-page pair, sorted by score descending.
  2. Work top-down through Tier 1, then Tier 2, until the weekly editorial capacity is filled.
  3. Log every intervention with a date so a later evaluation window can measure post-touch behavior directionally.
  4. Re-score weekly; expect Tier composition to shift as pages move through refresh cycles.
▲ CONFIDENCE STATEMENT

The tier boundaries are set from the score distribution on the grouped-split evaluation, not from a business SLA. If the editorial team has a hard capacity constraint (e.g., 30 pages/week), the top-K rows of the ranked output are the right cut — the tiers are a communication device, not a hard rule.
